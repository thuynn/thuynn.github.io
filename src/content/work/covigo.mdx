## The problem

During the pandemic, home isolation became the norm. Hospitals were overloaded and could not follow every patient,
while patients themselves had no simple way to know whether a reading was normal or dangerous.

Most existing health apps were built for clinicians: dense, full of medical terms and unforgiving to a scared,
first-time user. People ended up writing numbers in notebooks, searching symptoms on TikTok and hesitating to call
a doctor because they "didn't want to bother anyone".

**Goal:** a two-way monitoring tool. Patients log vitals in seconds; medical staff watch a real-time dashboard and
step in early.

## Understanding the users

Interviews pointed to two very different people with the same core need: _"tell me if I'm okay, and what to do if
I'm not"_.

- **Linh, 19, student** — anxious, isolating alone, learns from Google and TikTok. Needs reassurance and clear
  next steps, not data.
- **Minh, 28, office worker** — recovering, data-oriented, already owns a smartwatch and an SpO2 meter. Wants trends
  and a fast way to share them with his doctor.

## Mapping the journey

The journey maps exposed the moments where people drop off: not knowing where to start, forgetting to log on day
three, and panicking when a number looks wrong but nothing explains why.

<Figure src={slide07} alt="User journey map for Linh" wide />
<Figure src={slide09} alt="User journey map for Minh" caption="Each pain point becomes an opportunity and a concrete design solution." wide />

**What came out of it:**

| Pain point | Design decision |
| --- | --- |
| "I don't know where to start" | Illustrated onboarding, one task per screen |
| Forgetting to log after a few days | Two friendly reminders a day, logging in 2 taps |
| "Is 94% SpO2 bad?" | Color + icon status with a one-line explanation and a clear action |
| Not knowing who to call | One-tap "Call family / doctor" that sends an automatic health report |
| Manual typing for device owners | Smartwatch sync, manual input only as a fallback |

## Flows

Logging is the heart of the app, so the flow was designed to branch per vital — SpO2, temperature, heart rate,
breathing and cough status — and converge on a single confirmation that reports to medical staff.

<Figure src={slide11} alt="Covigo user flow" caption="Open app → home → log a vital (manual or device) → confirm → report to medical staff." />

## Brand & visual language

Trust and warmth had to coexist: a calm blue for reliability, an energetic orange for actions and encouragement.
Mulish keeps numbers legible at a glance, and a custom outline icon set makes medical concepts friendly.

## What I learned

- **Emotion is a requirement.** For anxious users, an explanation next to a number matters as much as the number.
- **Design the "not okay" path first.** The alert-and-act flow drove more decisions than the happy path.
- **Two personas, one system.** Progressive disclosure let the same screens serve Linh (reassurance) and Minh
  (trends) without two separate products.
